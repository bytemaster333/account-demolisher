---
title: SEP-41 allowances
description: How allowances are found from on-chain events, confirmed against the token contract, capped, and revoked under an exact-shape guard.
---

SEP-41 is Stellar's Soroban token interface. An allowance is a standing approval that lets a
spender move a holder's tokens up to a limit until an expiry ledger passes or the holder revokes
it.

`src/lib/soroban/allowances.ts` finds them and builds revokes. Two places use it: the allowance
viewer at `/allowances`, and the discovery step of a close, which plans one `RevokeAllowance` per
allowance it finds.

## The scan window

```
DEFAULT_SCAN_WINDOW_LEDGERS = EVENT_RETENTION_WINDOW_LEDGERS = 120_960
```

Soroban RPC keeps events for about seven days by default: `7 × 24 × 60 × 60 / 5 = 120,960`
ledgers at five seconds a ledger. Every event scan in the app looks back this far at most, and an
allowance whose last approve is older than the window cannot be found by an event scan. The allowance
viewer, the Review step and the status panel all state this seven-day limit.

## Scanning

The scan asks for contract events whose topics are `approve` and your address, with three topics or
four (`["approve", from, *]` and `["approve", from, *, *]`). The window is cut into slices of
10,000 ledgers, the most one `getEvents` request covers, and four slices run at once. A slice the
RPC refuses as too costly is split down to 625 ledgers. The scan stops at a 20-second budget,
inside the 30-second discovery timeout.

When the RPC rejects a start ledger older than it retains, the scan reads the floor from the
`ledger range: N - M` error, moves the start to the floor plus 60 ledgers, and retries, up to three
times.

A scan that does not cover the whole window reports what it covered and why it stopped (the
budget, the processing limit, an RPC error, or the page cap). The viewer shows the partial list
with that note. A close carries the partial list into the plan and warns that an older permission
can be missing, pointing you at the Allowances page.

## Folding events into allowances

Events arrive in on-chain order (ledger, transaction, operation, event), and the last approve seen
for a token-and-spender pair is the one in force, even when two land in the same ledger. Each pair
becomes one record with the amount and expiry ledger of that approve.

By default the scan leaves out:

- records whose expiry ledger has passed, and
- records whose latest approve set the amount to zero, which is how a revoke looks.

The approve data decodes from the SEP-41 tuple `(amount: i128, expiration_ledger: u32)`, or from a
map with `amount` and `live_until_ledger` or `expiration_ledger` keys. An event that does not decode
cleanly, or that names a different owner, is ignored.

### The per-contract cap

Any contract can emit approve events naming you, for spenders it chooses, and its `allowance()` is
its own code too. A contract with more than 20 spenders in the window is cut to its 20 most recent,
and the cut is reported with a note that the token is hostile. A Stellar Asset Contract is never
cut: it runs no token code, so each of its approve events is an approval you signed. The cap bounds
the confirm reads, the revokes a close plans, and the rows the viewer renders.

## On-chain confirmation

An approve event is evidence that an event was emitted, and a malicious contract can emit a
fabricated one. `confirmAllowancesOnChain` reads the token's own `allowance(from, spender)` for every
record, six at a time. The record's `onChainAmount` then holds one of three states:

| Value       | Meaning                 |
| ----------- | ----------------------- |
| `undefined` | Not confirmed           |
| `null`      | The confirm read failed |
| `bigint`    | The current allowance   |

A failed read keeps the row and marks it unconfirmed. `allowanceAmountMismatch` is true only when a
successful read differs from the event amount, and the viewer then shows the on-chain figure.

Both paths confirm. The viewer shows every confirmed row. Discovery for a close drops rows whose
on-chain read is zero (`withoutConfirmedZero`), because an allowance spent through `transfer_from`
or already revoked leaves nothing to revoke. When the confirm step fails as a whole, the close keeps
the event-derived list and records the failure as a discovery warning.

A hostile token's `allowance()` can answer non-zero for spenders it invented, so confirmation does
not filter fabricated rows on its own. The cap above and the revoke guard below bound what such a
token can make you sign.

## Revoking

A revoke is an approve set to zero:

```
approve(from, spender, 0, currentLedger)
```

The arguments are `[address(from), address(spender), i128(0), u32(currentLedger)]`. The expiry
argument is the current ledger sequence.

The transaction carries the Soroban inclusion fee of 100,000 stroops and a 300-second time bound,
and is assembled from simulation. An assembled fee bump is refused.

### Checks before signing

- **The signer is the owner.** SEP-41 requires the transaction source to be the approval's owner.
  The revoke re-reads the connector's public key and refuses when it changed:
  _Your wallet's active account changed. Switch back to the account that owns this address, or
  reconnect it, to revoke._ The viewer also requires a connected wallet whose key equals the
  address being viewed.
- **The call has exactly one shape.** `assertSafeRevokeInvocation`
  (`src/lib/soroban/revoke-guard.ts`) requires one `invokeHostFunction` operation calling
  `approve` on the expected contract with four arguments: the expected owner, the expected spender,
  an amount of exactly 0, and a ledger. Every authorization entry must use source-account
  credentials, have no sub-invocations, and authorize that same call. Anything else throws
  `Refusing to sign revoke: <reason>`. This blocks a hostile token from adding a nested transfer of
  your other balances to the authorization your signature covers.
- **The fee has a ceiling.** The transaction is held to the `untrusted-token` class in
  `src/lib/soroban/fee-cap.ts`: a resource fee of at most 2,000,000 stroops, at most 8,192 declared
  write bytes, and at most 16 read-write entries.

These checks are why `RevokeAllowance` is not held to the DeFi contract allow-list: the token
contract comes from your own allowance scan, and the call is pinned to a zero approval on it.

On the allowance viewer, building and signing are two steps (`prepareRevoke` and
`submitPreparedRevoke` in `src/lib/soroban/revoke.ts`), so the fee the signature authorizes is on
screen before anything is signed. The token's own code sizes a revoke's resources, and a
pasted-key connector signs without a prompt of its own, so for that connector the page is the only
place the fee appears.

Revokes for one account are signed one at a time: the next waits until the previous one is
included, or at most 90 seconds (`REVOKE_GATE_RELEASE_MS`) when its confirmation never settles,
and reads the sequence number from the same RPC. When the reviewed transaction can no longer land, because its sequence number is
stale or its time bound passed, it is rebuilt and checked again, and the rebuilt one is signed only
when its fee is no higher than the reviewed one. Otherwise `RevokeFeeChangedError` puts the rebuilt
revoke and its fee up for review, and nothing is signed. A row shows **Revoked** only after the
ledger confirms it.

In a close, a revoke that fails is skipped with a note, and the allowance stays on the token
contract. A merge does not remove allowances: they live in the token's storage.

## Held tokens

A separate scan in `src/lib/soroban/held-tokens.ts` finds standalone SEP-41 balances: it reads
`transfer` and `mint` events crediting the account in the same seven-day window, then probes
`balance(address)` on each emitting contract. The close moves those balances with `TransferAsIs`,
or sells them with `ConvertSorobanToXLM`. `assertSafeTransferInvocation` holds a transfer to exactly
`transfer(user, destination, amount)` the same way the revoke guard holds an approve, under the
`untrusted-transfer` fee class: a resource fee of at most 7,500,000 stroops, at most 16,384 declared
write bytes, and at most 24 read-write entries. A transfer that cannot be sent is skipped, and the
close result names the token as left behind on the closed account.
